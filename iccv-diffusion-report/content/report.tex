\begin{abstract}
Diffusion models generate images by learning to reverse a gradual corruption
process. This report connects that intuition to Gaussian forward transitions,
noise-prediction training, and iterative sampling. A scalar calculation and an
original five-level image sequence make the mechanism concrete. The main focus
is latent diffusion: a variational autoencoder compresses images, a conditional
denoiser models the compressed representation, and a decoder returns it to
pixels. Cross-attention and classifier-free guidance explain how prompts
influence generation. A published pixel-versus-latent inpainting comparison
supports a conditional efficiency advantage, while our analysis separates
spatial compression from faster sampling. Reconstruction error, imperfect
semantic control, data dependence, bias, and copyright concerns limit what
efficiency and visual realism alone establish.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
How can learning to remove noise enable a model to generate new images?
A denoiser trained on many images learns which structures are plausible at
different noise levels. Starting from random noise, repeated learned updates
can therefore produce a new sample from an approximation to the training
distribution. It need not recover a particular image that was previously
corrupted.

Pixel-space diffusion makes these updates over an entire image.
Latent diffusion instead separates \emph{representation learning} from
\emph{generative modeling}. The guiding question of this report is:
\emph{when does compression reduce the cost of diffusion while retaining
useful visual detail and conditional control?} We review DDPM
\cite{ho2020ddpm} and LDM \cite{rombach2022ldm}, then connect their mechanisms
to an evidence-supported comparison.

Our scope is explanatory and analytical. We compute a forward process locally;
we do not train a denoiser or claim to have run a pretrained image generator.
Results from the literature are explicitly identified in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\section{Background and Paper Review}\label{sec:background}
\subsection{Generative versus discriminative models}\label{sec:families}
A discriminative model estimates a label from an observation, for example
$p_\omega(y\mid x)$ for classifying an image $x$ as a cat.
Here $y$ is a label and $\omega$ denotes learned parameters.
A generative model represents a distribution such as $p_\theta(x)$ or
$p_\theta(x\mid y)$, allowing it to sample images, possibly conditioned on
a label or text. A classifier's confidence alone does not provide an image
sampling procedure.

\subsection{DDPM: forward corruption and learned reversal}\label{sec:ddpm}
Intuitively, the forward process slowly replaces structure with Gaussian
noise. The reverse model learns plausible transitions in the opposite
direction; it is not given the actual noise used to corrupt a test image.
For a vectorized image with $D$ scalar coordinates, DDPM specifies
\cite[Sec.~2]{ho2020ddpm}
\begin{align}
q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})
 &=\Normal\!\left(\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1},\,\beta_t\Id\right),\label{eq:forward}\\
x_t&=\sqrt{\ab_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\ab_t}\,\eps,
\quad \eps\sim\Normal(0,\Id),\label{eq:closed}\\
\alpha_t&=1-\beta_t,\qquad
\ab_t=\prod_{j=1}^{t}\alpha_j,\quad \ab_0=1.\nonumber
\end{align}
$x_0$ is a clean image sampled from the empirical data distribution
$p_{\rm data}$; $x_t\in\R^D$ is its noisy version. The integer
$t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}$ indexes noise level, not seconds.
$\beta_t\in(0,1)$ is a dimensionless per-step variance; $\Id$ is the
$D$-dimensional identity matrix. The \emph{schedule} is the sequence
$(\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_T)$, which controls how quickly signal disappears.
Equation~\eqref{eq:closed} samples a marginal directly, using one fresh
standard-normal vector $\eps$; it need not simulate every preceding step.

The noise predictor $\eps_\theta(x_t,t)\in\R^D$, with trainable parameters
$\theta$, minimizes the required simplified objective
\cite[Eq.~14]{ho2020ddpm}:
\begin{equation}
L_{\rm simple}=\E_{t,x_0,\eps}
\left[\|\eps-\eps_\theta(x_t,t)\|_2^2\right].\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
The expectation averages uniformly sampled timesteps, training images, and
independent Gaussian noise; $\|\cdot\|_2^2$ sums squared coordinate errors.
Training predicts the \emph{aggregate} noise in Eq.~\eqref{eq:closed}.
An epsilon-parameterized DDPM sampling update is
\begin{equation}
x_{t-1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}
\left(x_t-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\ab_t}}\eps_\theta(x_t,t)\right)
+\sigma_t u.\label{eq:reverse}
\end{equation}
Here $u\sim\Normal(0,\Id)$ and $\sigma_t\ge0$ is the chosen reverse standard deviation (e.g.,
$\sigma_t^2=\beta_t$ for $t>1$); the final step uses no added noise.
Sampling starts at $x_T\sim\Normal(0,\Id)$ when $\ab_T$ is sufficiently
small. DDPM's key contribution is effective noise-prediction training and
its connection to denoising score matching, not the invention of all
diffusion-based generative models.


\section{Method and Worked Example}\label{sec:method}
\subsection{A complete one-dimensional calculation}\label{sec:scalar}
For one step $t=1$, choose normalized $x_0=0.8$, variance $\beta_1=0.1$,
and Gaussian realization $\eps=-0.5$. This larger variance makes the
arithmetic visible; the image schedule below uses smaller steps.
Equation~\eqref{eq:forward} gives
\begin{align*}
\alpha_1&=1-0.1=0.9,\\
\sqrt{\alpha_1}\,x_0&=\sqrt{0.9}\,(0.8)=0.758947,\\
\sqrt{\beta_1}\,\eps&=\sqrt{0.1}\,(-0.5)=-0.158114,\\
x_1&=0.758947-0.158114=\boxed{0.600833}.
\end{align*}
Thus $q(x_1\mid x_0=0.8)=\Normal(0.758947,0.1)$: its mean is $0.758947$,
variance $0.1$, and standard deviation $\sqrt{0.1}=0.316228$.
Across independent draws, $\E[x_1\mid x_0]=\sqrt{0.9}\,x_0$ since
$\E[\eps]=0$. The realization $0.600833$ differs from that expectation;
the conditional variance is $\beta_1$.

\subsection{Five noise levels on an original small image}\label{sec:noise}
Our original $28\times28$ geometric house has foreground $+1$ and
background $-1$, without an external dataset.
The script \texttt{code/make\_figures.py} simulates one Markov trajectory
using Eq.~\eqref{eq:forward} with independent noise at every step.
It uses NumPy's default generator, seed $420$, $T=1000$, and
the linear schedule $\beta_t=10^{-4}+(t-1)(0.02-10^{-4})/999$,
following DDPM's schedule choice \cite[Sec.~4]{ho2020ddpm}.

\section{Latent Diffusion: Representation, Generation, and Control}\label{sec:ldm}
\subsection{Intuition: learn a useful compressed image space}\label{sec:vae}
An image contains both perceptually important structure and costly pixel
detail. LDM first learns an image representation, then models its distribution
with diffusion \cite[Secs.~3.1--3.2]{rombach2022ldm}.
The encoder is a learned analysis transform; it does not produce a smaller
JPEG or a text description. Its spatial tensor retains features that a
decoder can turn back into a plausible image.

For an RGB image $x_0\in\R^{H\times W\times3}$, a VAE encoder with parameters
$\phi$ predicts an approximate posterior \cite{kingma2014vae}:
\begin{align}
q_\phi(\tilde z\mid x_0)
 &=\Normal\!\left(\mu_\phi(x_0),\diag(\sigma_\phi^2(x_0))\right),\\
\tilde z&=\mu_\phi(x_0)+\sigma_\phi(x_0)\odot\xi,\quad
\xi\sim\Normal(0,\Id),\nonumber\\
\hat x_0&=D_\psi(\tilde z).\label{eq:vae}
\end{align}
$\mu_\phi$ and $\sigma_\phi>0$ are elementwise latent means and standard
deviations; $\diag$ forms a diagonal covariance, and $\odot$ is elementwise
multiplication. $\xi$ is \emph{encoder} noise, distinct from diffusion noise
$\eps$. $D_\psi$ is the decoder with parameters $\psi$; $\hat x_0$
is a reconstruction, not necessarily the original $x_0$.
Reparameterization moves randomness into $\xi$, allowing gradients to pass
through the encoder's outputs.

The tensor $\tilde z\in\R^{h\times w\times c}$ has $h=H/f$, $w=W/f$,
latent channels $c$, and spatial downsampling factor $f$.
For example, $256\times256\times3$ becomes $64\times64\times3$ for
$f=4,c=3$, a configuration in LDM's autoencoder study
\cite[Table~8]{rombach2022ldm}. The number of spatial positions falls
by $f^2=16$; channel count must also be considered when comparing storage.

\subsubsection*{What the autoencoder objective preserves}
For a textbook VAE, minimizing the negative evidence lower bound gives
\cite{kingma2014vae}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal J_{\rm VAE}&=
\E_{q_\phi(\tilde z\mid x_0)}
[-\log p_\psi(x_0\mid\tilde z)]\\
&+\KL\!\left(q_\phi(\tilde z\mid x_0)\,\|\,\Normal(0,\Id)\right).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:elbo}
\end{equation}
$p_\psi(x_0\mid\tilde z)$ is the decoder likelihood; its first term penalizes
poor reconstruction. $\KL$ is the nonnegative Kullback--Leibler divergence
in nats, encouraging a regular latent space. LDM's
\emph{KL-regularized} autoencoder is VAE-like but uses perceptual and
patch-adversarial reconstruction objectives with a weak KL penalty
\cite[Sec.~3.1]{rombach2022ldm}, rather than only a textbook pixel likelihood.
The perceptual term compares learned visual features; the adversarial term
encourages locally realistic texture. These can preserve perceptual quality
without guaranteeing exact pixel recovery.

The original LDM paper also studies \emph{VQ-regularized} autoencoders.
Consequently, LDM is broader than VAE plus diffusion. We focus on
the KL/VAE variant to satisfy Requirement 5. Crucially, the diffusion prior
learns the encoded data distribution: weak KL regularization does not make
that distribution exactly standard normal.


\subsection{Diffusion in latent space and the complete pipeline}\label{sec:pipeline}
After autoencoder training, its parameters are fixed for diffusion training.
Let $z_0=a\tilde z$, where $a>0$ is an optional fixed scale chosen to keep
latent magnitudes suitable for the noise schedule. Take $a=1$ when no
rescaling is used; decoding must undo the same scale.
The Gaussian construction of Section~\ref{sec:ddpm} now acts on
$d_z=hwc$ latent coordinates, not RGB pixels:
\begin{align}
z_t&=\sqrt{\ab_t}\,z_0+\sqrt{1-\ab_t}\,\eps_z,\quad
\eps_z\sim\Normal(0,\Id_{d_z}),\label{eq:latentnoise}\\
L_{\rm LDM}&=\E_{x_0,y,t,\xi,\eps_z}
\left[\|\eps_z-\eps_\theta(z_t,t,\tau_\eta(y))\|_2^2\right].
\label{eq:ldmloss}
\end{align}
$y$ is the condition (here a prompt), and $\tau_\eta$ is its encoder with
parameters $\eta$. $\eps_\theta$ is a time-conditioned U-Net that returns
a tensor of the same shape as $z_t$. Its down/up paths and skip connections
combine context with spatial detail. Equation~\eqref{eq:ldmloss} is the
conditional noise-prediction objective of LDM \cite[Eq.~3]{rombach2022ldm},
written to show stochastic VAE encoding explicitly. A deterministic encoder
or posterior-mean encoding omits $\xi$.

For generation, replace $x$ by $z$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:reverse} and use the
conditional predictor, or use a compatible sampler such as DDIM
\cite{song2021ddim}. Starting with latent Gaussian noise, the sampler
produces an approximate draw from the learned $p_\theta(z_0\mid y)$;
the final image is $\hat x_0=D_\psi(z_0/a)$.
This is why the decoder is not a diffusion denoiser: it maps a completed
latent into pixels, while the U-Net repeatedly predicts noise.

\textbf{One training update.} Encode a paired image and prompt; sample a
uniform timestep and Gaussian latent noise; construct $z_t$; predict that
noise and backpropagate Eq.~\eqref{eq:ldmloss} into the denoiser (and condition
encoder when trainable). No full reverse chain is needed for this update.
At inference, parameters stay fixed while the sampler repeatedly updates
the latent state. This distinction separates learning from generation.


\subsection{Cross-attention: how language reaches the denoiser}\label{sec:attention}
The prompt does not directly overwrite pixels. Instead, every spatial
feature can retrieve relevant information from prompt tokens.
For a prompt such as \emph{a red cube beside a blue sphere}, the text encoder
produces contextual token embeddings; the denoiser learns which embeddings
help its current spatial features. This interaction can occur at several
U-Net resolutions and timesteps \cite[Sec.~3.3]{rombach2022ldm}.

Use a \emph{row-vector} convention. At one U-Net layer, flatten its feature
map into $F\in\R^{n\times d_u}$, where $n$ is the number of spatial
positions and $d_u$ is its channel width. Let
$C=\tau_\eta(y)\in\R^{m\times d_y}$ contain $m$ token embeddings of
width $d_y$. One cross-attention head computes
\cite{vaswani2017attention,rombach2022ldm}
\begin{align}
Q&=FW_Q,\quad K=CW_K,\quad V=CW_V,\label{eq:qkv}\\
A&=\softmax\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_a}}\right),\qquad O=AV.
\label{eq:attention}
\end{align}
The learned projections have shapes
$W_Q\in\R^{d_u\times d_a}$,
$W_K\in\R^{d_y\times d_a}$, and
$W_V\in\R^{d_y\times d_v}$.
Thus $Q\in\R^{n\times d_a}$ contains \emph{queries from image features},
$K\in\R^{m\times d_a}$ contains \emph{keys from text}, and
$V\in\R^{m\times d_v}$ contains text values.
$d_a,d_v$ are positive integer head widths. A superscript $\top$ denotes
transposition. Softmax acts across the $m$ tokens for each spatial row,
making $A\in\R^{n\times m}$ nonnegative with row sums of one.
$O\in\R^{n\times d_v}$ supplies text-dependent features that are projected
back into the U-Net's residual stream.

\subsubsection*{A numerical attention example}
Take one spatial query $Q=(1,0)$, two keys
$K_1=(\sqrt2,0)$, $K_2=(0,0)$, and $d_a=d_v=2$.
The scaled logits are $(1,0)$, so
$A=(e/(e+1),1/(e+1))\approx(0.7311,0.2689)$, where $e$ is Euler's number.
For $V_1=(1,0)$ and $V_2=(0,1)$, the output is
$O\approx(0.7311,0.2689)$. This original toy example shows weighted retrieval,
not an observed attention map from an actual model.

Cross-attention differs from self-attention because queries and keys come
from different representations. It also differs from concatenating an image
condition channel-wise. Its weights express learned associations, not a
guaranteed object mask or proof that a prompt relation has been satisfied.


\subsection{Prompt control and classifier-free guidance}\label{sec:cfg}
Cross-attention supplies the condition; \emph{classifier-free guidance}
(CFG) adjusts how strongly a conditional prediction influences sampling
\cite{ho2022cfg}. Train the same network with the condition replaced by
a null condition $\varnothing$ with probability $p_{\rm drop}\in(0,1)$.
At inference define
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\eps_u&=\eps_\theta(z_t,t,\tau_\eta(\varnothing)),\\
\eps_c&=\eps_\theta(z_t,t,\tau_\eta(y)),\\
\eps_{\rm cfg}&=\eps_u+s(\eps_c-\eps_u).
\end{aligned}\label{eq:cfg}
\end{equation}
$s\ge0$ is a dimensionless guidance scale. Here $s=0$ uses the unconditional
prediction, $s=1$ gives ordinary conditional sampling, and $s>1$
extrapolates in the conditional direction. The CFG paper writes the
equivalent expression $(1+w)\eps_c-w\eps_u$; its parameter is
$w=s-1$. Stating the convention prevents confusing two scales with
different numerical baselines.

For an original scalar illustration, $\eps_u=0.2$, $\eps_c=0.1$ and
$s=3$ give $\eps_{\rm cfg}=-0.1$. Guidance is therefore an extrapolation,
not a convex average. Substituting it into the reverse update changes the
generation trajectory. Greater guidance can improve conditional fidelity
while reducing diversity; it does not ensure monotonically better quality
\cite{ho2022cfg}. Standard CFG requires two denoiser evaluations per step,
often batched together, adding compute and potentially peak memory.

\textbf{Practical control.} To inspect a prompt change, hold the checkpoint,
initial noise seed, sampler, step count, resolution, and $s$ fixed.
Change one attribute or relation at a time, such as \emph{red cube} to
\emph{green cube}. Evaluate whether the specified attribute changed and
whether unrelated content remained stable. A fixed seed improves
comparability, but changing text can still reorganize the entire image.
Counting, spatial relations, and binding attributes to the correct objects
require explicit checks; a plausible-looking result is insufficient.
Appendix~\ref{app:repro} gives a future protocol, not claimed prompt results.

\subsection{LDM paper: method, contributions, and differences}\label{sec:synthesis}
Rombach et al.\ \cite{rombach2022ldm} make three connected methodological
contributions relevant here. First, a reusable perceptual autoencoder
separates image compression from training a generative prior. Second,
diffusion on a moderately compressed spatial grid reduces repeated
high-resolution computation. Third, cross-attention provides a general
interface for conditions such as text or layouts. Their compression study
also shows why more compression is not automatically better: overly small
grids discard detail that the generative model cannot faithfully recover.

Compared with DDPM, LDM changes the \emph{representation} on which the
noise-prediction objective operates and introduces an autoencoding boundary;
it retains the central denoising principle. Compared with earlier
autoregressive latent generators, it uses iterative spatial denoising rather
than a chosen sequential token ordering. Compared with DDIM, it addresses
the cost of each state evaluation; DDIM addresses the sampling trajectory
and number of evaluations. These ideas can be combined. The original LDM
paper should also be distinguished from any particular later Stable
Diffusion checkpoint, dataset, text encoder, or user interface.

Our arithmetic gives sampling-throughput ratios
$0.97/0.26=3.73$ at $256^2$ and $0.34/0.07=4.86$ at $512^2$.
The epoch-time ratio is $20.66/7.66=2.70$ and training-throughput ratio
$0.32/0.11=2.91$. The reported FID is $9.53$ points lower, a relative
decrease of $38.5\%$; this percentage is \emph{not} a percentage increase
in perceptual quality.

FID (Fr\'echet Inception Distance) compares feature-distribution statistics
between generated and reference images \cite{heusel2017fid}. It does not
directly measure prompt adherence, fairness, factual correctness, or the
quality of every individual image.
The result supports an efficiency--quality advantage for this configuration
and training endpoint. Six equal epochs do not imply equal wall-clock
training budgets or equal convergence. Moreover, the table flags
GPU-setting/batch-size differences from the paper's Figure~7; its ratios
should not be universalized. Separate autoencoder pretraining is an
additional cost not isolated by this table. Inpainting uses spatial conditions, so this
experiment does not itself establish a text-prompt-control advantage.

\textbf{Where speed comes from.}
An analytical latency decomposition is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal T_{\rm gen}\approx
\mathcal T_{\rm text}
+K\,r\,\mathcal T_{\rm U\text{-}Net}
+\mathcal T_{\rm decode},\label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
where every $\mathcal T$ is measured in seconds, $K$ is the number of
sampling updates, and $r$ is the effective number of denoiser evaluations
per update ($1$ without standard CFG, $2$ with it, before batching effects).
The final term is absent in pixel diffusion. Latent compression mainly
reduces the cost of a U-Net evaluation; DDIM can reduce $K$
\cite{song2021ddim}. Fewer updates, higher guidance, and greater compression
are separate design choices with different quality consequences.

\textbf{Why a smaller state is not a guaranteed memory ratio.}
At $256^2$, RGB has $196{,}608$ coordinates; the illustrative
$64\times64\times3$ latent has $12{,}288$, giving a $16{:}1$ state-element
ratio. Generally it is $3f^2/c$.
At fixed feature width, spatial activation storage also benefits from fewer
positions, but model weights, attention, batching, and the full-resolution
decoder affect the peak. Therefore neither a $16\times$ runtime speedup nor
a $16\times$ total-memory reduction follows from this arithmetic.

\textbf{Quality and applicability.}
Moderate compression can make iterative generation practical at larger
resolutions. Tasks needing exact text, tiny structures, or pixel-faithful
reconstruction must also inspect autoencoder error. Stronger guidance and
lower FID cannot substitute for checking requested object attributes and
relations. These distinctions explain why the favorable inpainting result
is useful evidence without settling every application.


\subsection{Training data, bias, copyright, and limitations}\label{sec:ethics}
\textbf{Data dependence.}
The VAE learns what can be represented, the denoiser learns what is likely,
and the text encoder learns which language features connect to imagery.
Errors or underrepresentation at any stage can propagate to outputs.
Large image--text corpora such as LAION-5B illustrate the scale of this
dependency \cite{schuhmann2022laion}; they are not interchangeable with the
specific datasets used in every original LDM experiment.
Caption noise, duplicate images, language imbalance, and missing
provenance are distinct concerns. Our analytical recommendation is to
document dataset versions, sources, filters, and evaluation splits, and
audit duplicates and underrepresented concepts.

\textbf{Bias and prompt control.}
Bianchi et al.\ \cite{bianchi2023bias} report demographic stereotype
amplification in text-to-image systems, including prompts that do not
explicitly name demographic groups. Latent compression does not remove
these associations. Because guidance amplifies conditional preferences
and can reduce diversity, stronger prompt adherence should not be assumed
to imply fairer representation. Evaluation should compare distributions
across multiple prompts, groups, and seeds; prompt rewriting alone is
insufficient evidence of mitigation.

\textbf{Memorization and copyright.}
Carlini et al.\ \cite{carlini2023extract} demonstrate extraction of memorized
training examples from diffusion models. This establishes a possible
failure mode, not that every output is copied.
Dataset availability, permission to use underlying works, memorization
risk, and rights in an output are separate questions.
The U.S. Copyright Office's 2025 pre-publication analysis distinguishes
data collection, training, outputs, and licensing
\cite{usco2025training}; it is a U.S. policy source, not Vietnamese law.
Compression is no technical guarantee against reproduction.
Our recommendations are documented provenance, appropriate permissions,
deduplication, and similarity checks for sensitive use cases; none alone
certifies legal compliance.

\textbf{Limits of this report.}
Our original evidence covers a scalar calculation and one synthetic
forward trajectory. The model comparison is a published inpainting
experiment, not our own matched text-to-image benchmark. We have no
measured peak-memory values or prompt-adherence scores.
A future study should hold hardware, precision, batch size, sampler, step
count, guidance, resolution, and dataset fixed, report both autoencoder
and denoiser costs, and evaluate quality and control separately.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
Learning noise removal across corruption levels supplies a generative
sampling rule. Latent diffusion retains this principle while moving its
repeated computation into a learned spatial representation. The VAE
determines the balance between compression and reconstruction; the denoiser learns
the latent distribution; cross-attention and guidance connect prompts to
sampling. Published inpainting evidence supports a substantial efficiency
benefit for a concrete KL-regularized LDM-4 setting, with lower FID at the
reported endpoint. The broader conclusion is conditional: useful
compression can improve practicality, while reconstruction fidelity,
semantic control, data quality, bias, and rights concerns still require
their own evaluation.
