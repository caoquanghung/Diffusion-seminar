\begin{abstract}
How can learning to remove noise enable a model to generate new images?
We study this question through two papers. Denoising Diffusion
Probabilistic Models (DDPM) corrupt images with Gaussian noise, train a
network to predict that noise, and generate by denoising pure noise over
many small steps. Latent Diffusion Models (LDM) run the same process in
the compressed latent space of an autoencoder, which makes each step
cheaper, and condition it on text or other inputs through
cross-attention. We derive the forward process, DDPM's variational
objective, and its simplified noise-prediction loss, and illustrate
forward noising with a worked one-dimensional calculation and a
five-level image sequence. With pretrained checkpoints and no training of
our own, we compare pixel and latent generators on LSUN Churches (50 DDIM
steps, one T4 GPU). The latent model needs a median $2.44$~s per image
versus $6.10$~s, but its peak tensor allocation is higher, $1.68$ versus
$0.75$~GiB. Saved trajectories for five ImageNet classes show structure
emerging as noise is removed, and a small Stable Diffusion test shows that
a prompt edit can change an object's color without reversing its
left--right position. We relate these observations to published
results and to limits set by training data, copyright, and bias.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Generating a realistic image requires a model of the whole distribution
of natural images, including shape, texture, lighting, and spatial layout.
Diffusion models approach this task from an unusual direction. They first
destroy image structure by gradually adding Gaussian noise, and then learn
to undo the corruption \cite{sohl2015diffusion,ho2020ddpm}. Latent
diffusion extends the idea to compressed representations and underlies
text-to-image systems such as Stable Diffusion
\cite{rombach2022ldm,compvis2022sd14}.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Learning objective.} Explain forward noising
and reverse denoising, including why latent diffusion reduces
computational demands.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Guiding question.} How can learning to remove
noise enable a model to generate new images?

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{From denoising to generation.}
A denoiser repairs a given corrupted image, whereas a generator must
produce an image that never existed. A bridge exists because the
corruption is a known Gaussian process: it supplies training pairs at
every noise level, and a network trained on them learns statistical
structure shared across many images. Running the network from pure noise
then yields a new sample, provided that generation is split into many
small steps. A single network trained with squared error to map pure noise
to an image would output the conditional mean, approximately the average
training image, because $x_T$ carries almost no information about $x_0$
(Appendix~\ref{app:dsm}); small stochastic reverse steps avoid this.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Papers and scope.}
We center the report on two papers. DDPM \cite{ho2020ddpm} makes the 2015
diffusion framework \cite{sohl2015diffusion} practical by training a
network to predict the added noise, but it denoises at full image
resolution, so every sampling step is expensive. LDM
\cite{rombach2022ldm} runs the process in the latent space of a
separately trained autoencoder and adds cross-attention so that the
denoiser can be conditioned on text and other inputs.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Papers versus our own work.}
From the papers, we synthesize the forward/reverse construction, DDPM's
objective and score interpretation, and LDM's two-stage design; published
numbers are attributed to their sources (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}).
Our own material is a worked one-dimensional forward step, a five-level
noising sequence of a synthetic image, and experiments with pretrained
checkpoints: a timing and memory comparison of pixel and latent
generators on LSUN Churches, denoising trajectories for five ImageNet
classes, and a small prompt-control test with Stable Diffusion v1.4. These
experiments use few samples and measure neither FID nor bias, so they are
illustrations rather than benchmarks.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Organization.}
Section~\ref{sec:background} gives background, Section~\ref{sec:ddpm}
develops DDPM with two worked examples, and Section~\ref{sec:ldm}
explains LDM. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports our experiments and
published evidence and discusses limits from training data, copyright,
and bias; Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Background and Paper Review}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Generative versus discriminative models}\label{sec:families}
The fundamental distinction between discriminative and generative models lies in what they aim to learn from data. 
A discriminative model learns to predict a target $y$ given an observation $x$, typically by estimating $p_\omega(y\mid x)$ or learning a decision boundary between classes. 
For instance, a cat classifier only needs to identify features that distinguish cats from other objects, without explicitly modeling the distribution of natural images.
In contrast, a generative model aims to capture the underlying data distribution, 
such as $p_\theta(x)$ or the conditional distribution $p_\theta(x\mid y)$. 
Rather than merely distinguishing categories, it must model the statistical structure and variability of the data, including factors such as shape, texture, lighting, and spatial relationships. This enables the model to generate new samples that resemble the training data, potentially conditioned on labels or text descriptions.
Consequently, generative modeling generally requires capturing richer information about the data than is necessary for classification.

\subsection{Qualitative Comparison of Generative Models}\label{sec:generator-comparison}
GANs, autoregressive models, and diffusion models differ in how they
learn and sample the data distribution.
A \emph{GAN} trains a generator to transform random noise into images while
a discriminator learns to distinguish generated images from real ones
\cite{goodfellow2014gan}. Sampling typically takes one generator pass,
but training requires balancing the two competing networks.
An \emph{autoregressive model} generates an image one pixel or token at a
time, predicting each from the previously generated values
\cite{oord2016pixelrnn}. This gives an explicit factorization of the image
distribution, although sequential generation can be costly.
A \emph{diffusion model} learns to reverse gradual noise corruption and
generates an image by repeatedly denoising an initially random state
\cite{ho2020ddpm}. It refines the whole state across multiple steps,
requiring repeated network evaluations during sampling.
Figure~\ref{fig:families} in Appendix~\ref{app:familycomparison}
compares the generative mechanisms of these three families.

\subsection{Historical context}\label{sec:history}

The foundations of diffusion-based generative modeling were established
by Sohl-Dickstein et al.\ \cite{sohl2015diffusion}, who introduced a
probabilistic framework inspired by nonequilibrium thermodynamics.
Their approach consists of a forward diffusion process that gradually
destroys the structure of the data by adding noise, followed by a
learned reverse process that reconstructs samples from noise.
By decomposing a complex generative transformation into a sequence
of small, tractable transitions, this framework enables likelihood-based
training and probabilistic inference. However, generating samples
requires many sequential reverse steps, making inference computationally
expensive.

An alternative perspective was developed by Song and Ermon
\cite{song2019score} through score-based generative modeling.
Instead of directly modeling the data probability density, their
approach estimates its score function, defined as the gradient of
the log-density. To address difficulties in estimating scores when
data lie near low-dimensional manifolds, they introduced multiple
levels of Gaussian noise and trained a noise-conditional score
network. Samples are then generated using annealed Langevin dynamics,
which progressively moves samples toward high-density regions
as the noise level decreases.

Ho et al.\ \cite{ho2020ddpm} subsequently introduced Denoising
Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM), establishing a connection
between diffusion probabilistic modeling and denoising score matching.
Their formulation parameterizes the reverse process through a neural
network that predicts the Gaussian noise added at each diffusion
timestep.

Despite their strong generative performance, conventional diffusion
models operate directly in high-dimensional pixel space, resulting
in substantial training and sampling costs. To address this limitation,
Rombach et al.\ \cite{rombach2022ldm} proposed Latent Diffusion Models
(LDM). This architecture incorporates cross-attention mechanisms to support flexible conditioning,
including text-guided image generation.
LDM builds this latent space with a variational autoencoder, reviewed next.

\subsection{Variational autoencoders}\label{sec:vae-background}
A variational autoencoder (VAE) \cite{kingma2014vae} is a latent-variable
generative model. It assumes that an image $x_0\in\R^D$ is produced from
a latent vector $\tilde z$ drawn from a simple prior:
\begin{equation}
p(\tilde z)=\Normal(0,\Id),\qquad x_0\sim p_\psi(x_0\mid\tilde z),
\label{eq:vaegen}
\end{equation}
where the \emph{decoder} $p_\psi$ is a neural network with parameters
$\psi$. The likelihood
$p_\psi(x_0)=\int p(\tilde z)\,p_\psi(x_0\mid\tilde z)\,d\tilde z$ is
intractable, so an \emph{encoder} with parameters $\phi$ approximates the
posterior over latents:
\begin{align}
q_\phi(\tilde z\mid x_0)
 &=\Normal\!\left(\mu_\phi(x_0),\diag(\sigma_\phi^2(x_0))\right),\nonumber\\
\tilde z&=\mu_\phi(x_0)+\sigma_\phi(x_0)\odot\xi,\quad
\xi\sim\Normal(0,\Id).\label{eq:vaeenc}
\end{align}
The encoder outputs latent means $\mu_\phi$ and positive standard
deviations $\sigma_\phi$; $\diag$ forms a diagonal covariance and $\odot$
denotes elementwise multiplication. This reparameterization places the
randomness in $\xi$, so gradients flow through the sampled latent.
Encoder and decoder are trained jointly by minimizing the negative
evidence lower bound (ELBO):
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal J_{\rm VAE}
&=\E_{q_\phi(\tilde z\mid x_0)}[-\log p_\psi(x_0\mid\tilde z)]\\
&\quad+\KL\!\left(q_\phi(\tilde z\mid x_0)\,\|\,\Normal(0,\Id)\right)
\ \ge\ -\log p_\psi(x_0).
\end{aligned}\label{eq:elbo}
\end{equation}
The first term rewards reconstruction; the KL term pulls every encoding
toward the prior, so that decoding a fresh draw $\tilde z\sim\Normal(0,\Id)$
generates a new image. With a Gaussian decoder, the reconstruction term is
a pixel-wise squared error; such pixel losses tend to average over
plausible fine details and yield blurry images \cite[Sec.~3.1]{rombach2022ldm}.
The diffusion bound of Section~\ref{sec:variational} follows the same
variational idea, with the noisy states $x_{1:T}$ as latents and a fixed,
rather than learned, encoder $q$.


\section{Method: DDPM and the Diffusion Framework}\label{sec:ddpm}\label{sec:method}
We first introduce the forward/reverse construction established by
Sohl-Dickstein et al.\ \cite{sohl2015diffusion}, then focus on DDPM's
variational objective, noise-prediction parameterization, simplified loss,
and sampling \cite{ho2020ddpm}. We use DDPM's $x_t,\alpha_t,\ab_t$
notation throughout. The original 2015 training bound is retained in
Appendix~\ref{app:originalbound}.

\subsection{Forward process, Gaussian noise, and schedule}\label{sec:forward}
A clean image $x_0\in\R^D$ is gradually corrupted by a Gaussian Markov
chain. The Gaussian branch of the 2015 model uses
\cite[Sec.~2.1, Table~C.1]{sohl2015diffusion}
\begin{align}
q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})
 &=\Normal\!\left(\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1},\,\beta_t\Id\right),\label{eq:forward}\\
x_t&=\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1}+\sqrt{\beta_t}\,\eps^{(t)},\nonumber\\
\eps^{(t)}&\sim\Normal(0,\Id),\qquad
\alpha_t=1-\beta_t,\quad\ab_t=\prod_{j=1}^t\alpha_j.\nonumber
\end{align}
Here $D$ counts normalized image coordinates, $\Id$ is the identity
matrix, and $t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}$ is a step index, not physical time.
The innovations $\eps^{(t)}$ are independent across steps and coordinates.
The dimensionless variance $\beta_t\in(0,1)$ simultaneously attenuates
the previous signal by $\sqrt{1-\beta_t}$ and sets the new noise standard
deviation $\sqrt{\beta_t}$. The schedule $(\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_T)$
controls how rapidly structure is lost.

The standard Gaussian is stationary under this kernel: if a state has
covariance $\Id$ and is standard normal, the next state's covariance is
$(1-\beta_t)\Id+\beta_t\Id=\Id$. Repeated small steps can therefore
transform a complex image distribution toward a simple Gaussian prior.
In the 2015 Gaussian experiments, $\beta_1$ is fixed to a small value
while $\beta_2,\ldots,\beta_T$ are learned through the variational bound;
the paper also notes that a fixed schedule can work comparably
\cite[Sec.~2.4.1]{sohl2015diffusion}. The Gaussian kernel's form is known
even when its rates are optimized.

Repeated Gaussian composition gives, with $\ab_0=1$,
\begin{equation}
x_t=\sqrt{\ab_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\ab_t}\,\eps,
\qquad\eps\sim\Normal(0,\Id).\label{eq:closed}
\end{equation}
This is the same Gaussian marginal later used by DDPM, written in its
notation; it is not a new forward-process invention. The aggregate noise
$\eps$ differs from an individual innovation $\eps^{(t)}$.
For small $\ab_T$, the terminal distribution approaches standard normal;
a finite schedule need not make that equality exact.

\subsection{Reverse model and sampling}\label{sec:reversemodel}

Let $p_{\rm data}(x_0)$ denote the unknown data distribution.
The original diffusion model learns a finite reverse Markov chain,
starting from a simple Gaussian prior
$p(x_T)=\Normal(0,\Id)$ \cite[Sec.~2.2]{sohl2015diffusion}:
\begin{align}
p_\theta(x_{0:T})
&=p(x_T)\prod_{t=1}^T p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t),
\nonumber\\
p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)
&=\Normal\!\left(
\mu_\theta(x_t,t),\Sigma_\theta(x_t,t)
\right).
\label{eq:reversemodel}
\end{align}
Although the true reverse conditional depends on the unknown data
distribution and is not necessarily Gaussian, sufficiently small
diffusion steps motivate approximating each reverse transition
with a Gaussian distribution.

For learned intermediate transitions, neural functions
$f_\mu$ and $f_\Sigma$ parameterize the mean
$\mu_\theta(x_t,t)$ and covariance $\Sigma_\theta(x_t,t)$,
respectively. The mean specifies the expected previous state,
while the covariance captures uncertainty in the reverse transition.
Unlike DDPM's noise-prediction parameterization
(Section~\ref{sec:parameterization}), the original model directly
estimates these reverse-distribution parameters.

To generate a sample, the model draws
$x_T\sim\Normal(0,\Id)$ and iteratively applies the
reverse transitions:
\begin{equation}
x_{t-1}
=\mu_\theta(x_t,t)
+\Sigma_\theta(x_t,t)^{1/2}u_t,
\quad u_t\sim\Normal(0,\Id).
\label{eq:originalsampling}
\end{equation}
For diagonal covariance, the matrix square root corresponds
to elementwise standard deviations.
The original formulation additionally specifies a boundary
convention for the final transition at $t=1$
\cite[App.~B.2]{sohl2015diffusion}, detailed in
Appendix~\ref{app:originalbound}.
Through these successive stochastic transitions, the model
transforms Gaussian noise into samples approximating
$p_{\rm data}$, without requiring a clean input image.


Figure~\ref{fig:ddpm} shows the architecture of both flows: a Gaussian
kernel corrupts each forward state, while a neural model parameterizes
the reverse distribution from which the previous state is sampled.
Appendix~\ref{app:pseudocode} gives step-by-step pseudocode for forward
noising and the 2015 Gaussian reverse sampler
(Algorithms~\ref{alg:forward} and~\ref{alg:reverse2015}).

\subsection{DDPM: From Variational Training to Noise and Score Prediction}
\label{sec:variational}
\label{sec:parameterization}
\label{sec:objective}
\label{sec:reverse}
\label{sec:scorebridge}

\paragraph{Variational training and Gaussian posterior targets.}
DDPM inherits the forward/reverse construction above and its
likelihood-based training \cite{ho2020ddpm}. Since computing $p_\theta(x_0)$ requires
marginalizing over all intermediate states, training minimizes
a variational upper bound on the negative log likelihood
\cite[Eq.~5]{ho2020ddpm}:
\begin{equation}
L_{\rm VLB}
=L_T+\sum_{t=2}^{T}L_{t-1}+L_0.
\label{eq:vlbcomponents}
\end{equation}
Here, $L_T$ matches the terminal forward distribution to
the Gaussian prior, $L_0$ represents the final reconstruction
likelihood, and each intermediate term $L_{t-1}$ measures
the KL divergence between the learned reverse transition
$p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)$ and the forward posterior
$q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)$.

Although the unconditional reverse distribution is unknown,
conditioning on the clean training image $x_0$ gives a
tractable Gaussian posterior:
\begin{equation}
q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)
=\Normal(\tilde{\mu}_t,\tilde{\beta}_t\Id),
\qquad t>1.
\label{eq:forwardposterior}
\end{equation}
This provides an explicit training target for the reverse
distribution without requiring the data density itself.
The original diffusion framework learns both the reverse
mean and covariance. DDPM simplifies this formulation by
fixing the forward noise schedule and choosing a fixed
reverse variance $\Sigma_\theta=\sigma_t^2\Id$.

Under this assumption, the intermediate Gaussian KL reduces,
up to a constant independent of $\theta$, to mean matching
\cite[Eq.~8]{ho2020ddpm}:
\begin{equation}
L_{t-1}
=\E_q\left[
\frac{\|\tilde{\mu}_t-\mu_\theta(x_t,t)\|_2^2}
{2\sigma_t^2}
\right]+C.
\label{eq:meanloss}
\end{equation}
Thus, minimizing the variational bound requires learning
the posterior mean of each reverse transition.
Appendix~\ref{app:originalbound} gives the full Gaussian KL, including
the covariance terms that become constant under this assumption.

\paragraph{From mean prediction to noise prediction.}
Rather than predicting this mean directly, DDPM exploits
the closed-form marginal of Eq.~\eqref{eq:closed}
(Appendix~\ref{app:forwardmarginal}).
Substituting the expression for $x_0$ into the Gaussian
posterior mean gives \cite[Eqs.~10--11]{ho2020ddpm}:
\begin{align}
\tilde{\mu}_t
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}
\left(x_t-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}
\eps\right),
\label{eq:posteriormean-noise}\\
\mu_\theta(x_t,t)
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}
\left(x_t-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}
\eps_\theta(x_t,t)\right).
\label{eq:ddpmmean}
\end{align}

The second expression replaces the sampled Gaussian noise
with a neural prediction $\eps_\theta(x_t,t)$.
Consequently, predicting noise is an alternative
parameterization of the reverse mean, rather than a
different probabilistic model.

\paragraph{Connection to denoising score matching.}
This parameterization also connects DDPM to score-based generative
modeling (Section~\ref{sec:history}) \cite{song2019score}. The score of the noisy
data distribution $q_t(x_t)$ is defined as
$\nabla_{x_t}\log q_t(x_t)$, which points toward regions
of increasing probability density.
Appendix~\ref{app:scorebackground} provides background on scores and
Gaussian perturbations.

Because the forward corruption is Gaussian, the
conditional score can be written as
\begin{equation}
\nabla_{x_t}\log q(x_t\mid x_0)
=-\frac{\eps}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}.
\end{equation}
Taking the conditional expectation over clean images
given $x_t$ yields the marginal score:
\begin{equation}
\nabla_{x_t}\log q_t(x_t)
=-\frac{\E[\eps\mid x_t]}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}.
\end{equation}

Under squared-error training, the optimal noise predictor
estimates $\E[\eps\mid x_t]$. Appendix~\ref{app:dsm} derives this
MSE optimum and the conditional-to-marginal score identity;
Appendix~\ref{app:noiseinterpretation} explains why the estimate need
not recover the particular noise draw of a training example.
Therefore, the learned
noise predictor implicitly estimates the noisy-data score:
\begin{equation}
s_\theta(x_t,t):=
-\frac{\eps_\theta(x_t,t)}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}
\approx\nabla_{x_t}\log q_t(x_t).
\label{eq:scoreestimate}
\end{equation}
Substituting this relation into Eq.~\eqref{eq:ddpmmean}
gives an equivalent score-based expression:
\begin{equation}
\mu_\theta(x_t,t)
=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}
\left(x_t+\beta_t s_\theta(x_t,t)\right).
\end{equation}
Appendix~\ref{app:reversemean} derives the corresponding identity for
the true reverse conditional mean and clarifies the Gaussian approximation.

Hence, reverse-mean prediction, noise prediction, and
score estimation are closely related representations
of the same denoising function. This connection explains
why DDPM can be interpreted through denoising score
matching while retaining its probabilistic formulation.

\paragraph{Weighted and simplified noise objectives.}
Substituting the noise parameterization into the
Gaussian mean-matching loss gives
\cite[Eq.~12]{ho2020ddpm}:
\begin{align}
L_{t-1}
&=\E_{x_0,\eps}\left[
w_t\|\eps-\eps_\theta(x_t,t)\|_2^2
\right]+C,
\label{eq:weightedloss}\\
w_t
&=\frac{\beta_t^2}
{2\sigma_t^2\alpha_t(1-\bar{\alpha}_t)},
\qquad t>1.
\label{eq:noiseweight}
\end{align}
This weighted noise-prediction objective corresponds
to the intermediate terms of the variational bound; at each noise level
it is a rescaled score-matching loss (Appendix~\ref{app:dsm}).

DDPM further simplifies training by dropping the
timestep-dependent weights:
\begin{equation}
L_{\rm simple}
=\E_{t,x_0,\eps}
\left[
\|\eps-\eps_\theta(x_t,t)\|_2^2
\right].
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
Here, $t$ is sampled uniformly, and each noisy input
$x_t$ is constructed directly from a clean training
image and sampled Gaussian noise.

Removing $w_t$ changes the relative emphasis placed
on different noise levels. Ho et al.\ report improved
sample quality under this simplified objective, while
the original variational objective provides better
likelihood-based codelengths
\cite[Secs.~3.4, 4.1--4.2]{ho2020ddpm}, an empirical
trade-off rather than a general guarantee.

Note that $L_{\rm simple}$ is a surrogate, not the
complete bound: it omits the decoder term $L_0$ and
regresses noise at all timesteps, including $t=1$.

\paragraph{Reverse sampling.}
Once trained, the same noise predictor determines the
reverse sampling process. DDPM uses a fixed reverse
variance, with two choices investigated by Ho et al.\
\cite[Sec.~3.2]{ho2020ddpm}:
\begin{equation}
\sigma_t^2=\beta_t
\quad\text{or}\quad
\sigma_t^2=\tilde{\beta}_t
=\frac{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}
{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\beta_t,
\qquad t>1.
\label{eq:ddpmvariance}
\end{equation}

Starting from $x_T\sim\Normal(0,\Id)$, the model
iteratively samples \cite[Algorithm~2]{ho2020ddpm}:
\begin{equation}
x_{t-1}
=\mu_\theta(x_t,t)+\sigma_tu_t,
\quad u_t\sim\Normal(0,\Id),
\qquad t>1.
\label{eq:reverse}
\end{equation}
At $t=1$, the original DDPM sampler sets the added
noise to zero and outputs $\mu_\theta(x_1,1)$.
Appendix~\ref{app:pseudocode} gives forward/backward pseudocode and
distinguishes this endpoint from the original 2015 sampler.
In score form, each step adds a learned density-increasing direction
and fresh noise, resembling Langevin dynamics.


\subsection{A complete one-dimensional calculation}\label{sec:scalar}
For one step $t=1$, choose normalized $x_0=0.8$, variance $\beta_1=0.1$,
and Gaussian realization $\eps=-0.5$. This larger variance makes the
arithmetic visible; the image schedule below uses smaller steps.
Equation~\eqref{eq:forward} gives
\begin{align*}
\alpha_1&=1-0.1=0.9,\\
\sqrt{\alpha_1}\,x_0&=\sqrt{0.9}\,(0.8)=0.758947,\\
\sqrt{\beta_1}\,\eps&=\sqrt{0.1}\,(-0.5)=-0.158114,\\
x_1&=0.758947-0.158114=\boxed{0.600833}.
\end{align*}
Thus $q(x_1\mid x_0=0.8)=\Normal(0.758947,0.1)$: its mean is $0.758947$,
variance $0.1$, and standard deviation $\sqrt{0.1}=0.316228$.
Across independent draws, $\E[x_1\mid x_0]=\sqrt{0.9}\,x_0$ since
$\E[\eps]=0$. The realization $0.600833$ differs from that expectation;
the conditional variance is $\beta_1$.

\subsection{Five noise levels on an original small image}\label{sec:noise}
We apply Eq.~\eqref{eq:forward} step by step to a synthetic
$28\times28$ image of a house, with foreground $+1$ and background $-1$.
Following DDPM \cite[Sec.~4]{ho2020ddpm}, we use $T=1000$ and a linear
schedule increasing from $\beta_1=10^{-4}$ to $\beta_T=0.02$.
Figure~\ref{fig:noise} shows one sampled trajectory at five timesteps.

Values are clipped to $[-1,1]$ for display only.}\label{fig:noise}

In terms of Eq.~\eqref{eq:closed}, the signal-to-noise ratio

\section{LDM: Latent Space and Efficient Generation}\label{sec:ldm}
\subsection{Why move from pixels to a latent representation?}\label{sec:synthesis}
The diffusion framework supplies a way to generate images, but repeatedly processing a
high-resolution pixel grid is expensive. LDM separates learning a useful
image representation from learning its generative distribution
\cite[Secs.~3.1--3.2]{rombach2022ldm}. Its first stage compresses images;
its second stage applies diffusion to the resulting spatial tensors.

For RGB input $x_0\in\R^{H\times W\times3}$, let
$\tilde z\in\R^{h\times w\times c}$, where $h=H/f$, $w=W/f$,
$f\ge1$ is the spatial downsampling factor, and $c$ is the latent channel
count. For example, an LDM configuration with $f=4,c=3$ maps
$256\times256\times3$ to $64\times64\times3$
\cite[Table~8]{rombach2022ldm}. This reduces the number of spatial positions
by $16$, but the end-to-end runtime and memory depend on more than the
state shape.

\subsection{Perceptual image compression}\label{sec:vae}
LDM's first stage is an autoencoder with encoder $\mathcal E_\phi$ and
decoder $D_\psi$. It starts from the VAE of
Section~\ref{sec:vae-background}, but its only job is to compress images
\cite[Sec.~3.1]{rombach2022ldm}:
\begin{equation}
\tilde z=\mathcal E_\phi(x_0)\in\R^{h\times w\times c},\qquad
\hat x_0=D_\psi(\tilde z).\label{eq:vae}
\end{equation}
Unlike the flat vector of Eq.~\eqref{eq:vaegen}, $\tilde z$ keeps a
spatial layout that a convolutional U-Net \cite{ronneberger2015unet} can
process. In the KL variant below, $\mathcal E_\phi$ samples $\tilde z$ with
the reparameterization of Eq.~\eqref{eq:vaeenc}; this posterior noise $\xi$
differs from the diffusion noise added in the second stage.

\paragraph{Training objective.} Instead of the pixel likelihood in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:elbo}, the autoencoder combines three ingredients
\cite[Sec.~3.1]{rombach2022ldm}. A \emph{perceptual loss}
\cite{zhang2018lpips} compares deep-network features, so a reconstruction
must look like the input rather than match every pixel. A
\emph{patch-based adversarial loss} \cite{isola2017pix2pix,esser2021vqgan}
lets a discriminator judge local patches, pushing textures toward realism
and avoiding the blur of pixel-only losses. A light \emph{regularizer}
prevents latents with arbitrarily high variance.

\paragraph{Two regularizers.} \emph{KL-reg.} adds a weak KL penalty toward
$\Normal(0,\Id)$, as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:elbo} but with a small weight.
\emph{VQ-reg.} uses a vector-quantization layer \cite{oord2017vqvae}: each
latent vector $\tilde z_{ij}\in\R^c$ is replaced by the nearest entry of a
learned codebook $\mathcal Z\subset\R^c$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{VQ}(\tilde z)_{ij}=\arg\min_{e\in\mathcal Z}\|\tilde z_{ij}-e\|_2.
\label{eq:vq}
\end{equation}
LDM places this layer \emph{inside the decoder}: the model is a VQGAN
\cite{esser2021vqgan} whose quantization step is absorbed by $D_\psi$
\cite[Sec.~3.1]{rombach2022ldm}. The diffusion model therefore operates
on the continuous, pre-quantization latent, and quantization happens
only when a latent is decoded.

\paragraph{Why absorb quantization into the decoder?} The choice brings
four benefits.
(i)~\emph{Gaussian diffusion stays well defined.} Forward noising
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:latentnoise}) and noise regression require a continuous
space; Gaussian noise cannot be added to discrete codebook indices without
switching to a different, discrete diffusion process. Both variants expose
a continuous latent, so one denoiser and sampler design serves both.
(ii)~\emph{Tolerance to sampling error.} A generated latent need not land
exactly on codebook vectors; the decoder snaps each position to its
nearest code, projecting small residual denoising errors back onto latents
the decoder was trained on. This is our interpretation of the design, not
a claim tested in the paper.
(iii)~\emph{Spatial structure and mild compression.} VQGAN-style pipelines
model token indices autoregressively in an arbitrary 1D order, ignoring
much of the 2D structure of the latent. A U-Net models the 2D latent
directly, so LDM can use mild factors $f=4$--$8$ and keep reconstructions
faithful \cite[Sec.~3.1]{rombach2022ldm}.
(iv)~\emph{Bounded latent scale.} The codebook bounds latent magnitudes
without tuning a KL weight, whereas KL latents are rescaled by the fixed
factor $a$ of Section~\ref{sec:pipeline} \cite{rombach2022ldm}.
Empirically, VQ-regularized LDMs sometimes reach better sample quality even
though their reconstructions slightly trail the KL autoencoders
\cite[Sec.~4, Table~8]{rombach2022ldm}. Our experiments use both: the
ImageNet model is VQ-regularized with $f=4$, and the LSUN Churches model
is KL-regularized with $f=8$ (Table~\ref{tab:colab-setup}).

\paragraph{Diffusion replaces the VAE prior.} Neither regularizer makes
clean latents standard normal, so LDM learns their distribution with
diffusion (Section~\ref{sec:pipeline}) instead of decoding
$\tilde z\sim\Normal(0,\Id)$. Reconstruction quality limits the whole
generator: excessive compression may erase fine text or small objects.

\subsection{Diffusion training and sampling in latent space}\label{sec:pipeline}
The trained autoencoder is frozen while the latent denoiser is trained. Let $z_0=a\tilde z$, with optional fixed scale
$a>0$ to match latent magnitudes to the noise schedule; $a=1$ means no
rescaling. Diffusion acts on $d_z=hwc$ latent coordinates:
\begin{align}
z_t&=\sqrt{\ab_t}\,z_0+\sqrt{1-\ab_t}\,\eps_z,\quad
\eps_z\sim\Normal(0,\Id_{d_z}),\label{eq:latentnoise}\\
L_{\rm LDM}&=\E_{x_0,y,t,\xi,\eps_z}
\left[\|\eps_z-\eps_\theta(z_t,t,\tau_\eta(y))\|_2^2\right].
\label{eq:ldmloss}
\end{align}
The condition $y$ is encoded by $\tau_\eta$ with parameters $\eta$
(Section~\ref{sec:attention}). Eq.~\eqref{eq:ldmloss} follows
LDM \cite[Eq.~3]{rombach2022ldm} with the stochastic encoding made explicit.

For generation, start with latent Gaussian noise and repeatedly apply a
reverse sampler with the conditional predictor. Samplers such as DDIM
\cite{song2021ddim} reduce the number of steps, complementing the cheaper
latent steps. Decode the
final latent once as $D_\psi(z_0/a)$, undoing the training scale.

\subsection{Conditioning and the complete pipeline}\label{sec:attention}\label{sec:cfg}
LDM is not specific to text. It conditions the denoiser on an input $y$
from an arbitrary modality through a domain-specific encoder $\tau_\eta$
\cite[Sec.~3.3]{rombach2022ldm}.

\subsubsection{Conditioning through cross-attention}\label{sec:crossattn}
The encoder maps the condition to a sequence of $m$ token embeddings,
$C=\tau_\eta(y)\in\R^{m\times d_y}$, and cross-attention layers inserted
into the U-Net at several resolutions inject $C$ into the denoiser
$\eps_\theta(z_t,t,\tau_\eta(y))$ \cite[Sec.~3.3]{rombach2022ldm}. For
text, LDM trains a transformer $\tau_\eta$ jointly with the denoiser,
whereas Stable Diffusion v1.4 uses a frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 text encoder
\cite{radford2021clip,compvis2022sd14}. We use text as the running
example below.

At one layer, flatten the U-Net feature map into $F\in\R^{n\times d_u}$,
one row per spatial position. A cross-attention head
\cite{vaswani2017attention} computes
\begin{align}
Q&=FW_Q,\quad K=CW_K,\quad V=CW_V,\label{eq:qkv}\\
A&=\softmax\!\left(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_a}\right),\qquad O=AV,
\label{eq:attention}
\end{align}
with learned projections $W_Q,W_K,W_V$ (Figure~\ref{fig:attention}).
\emph{Queries come from the image; keys and values come from the condition.}
Row $i$ of $A\in\R^{n\times m}$ is a probability distribution over tokens:
it measures how well the query of position $i$ matches each token key,
with the $\sqrt{d_a}$ scaling keeping the softmax from saturating. Row $i$
of $O$ is the corresponding weighted average of token values; it is
projected back to $d_u$ channels and added to the feature map through a
residual connection. Each location therefore retrieves the words relevant
to it; a region forming a bus can draw on the tokens ``red'' and ``bus''.
Several heads run in parallel and their outputs are concatenated
\cite{vaswani2017attention}. Because $A$ is recomputed from the noisy
features at every layer and step, the prompt steers the denoising
predictions throughout sampling; it is not pasted onto the decoded image.

Cross-attention mixing is soft: an attribute can attach to the wrong object, or a token can be
neglected \cite{chefer2023attend}; Section~\ref{sec:prompt-results}
observes such failures. Matrix shapes and a toy calculation are in
Appendix~\ref{app:attention}.

Classifier-free guidance can strengthen conditional influence by using
$\eps_u+s(\eps_c-\eps_u)$, where $\eps_u,\eps_c$ are unconditional and
conditional predictions and $s\ge0$ is the guidance scale
\cite{ho2022cfg}; $s=1$ is ordinary conditional sampling. Stronger
guidance trades diversity for fidelity but does not guarantee correct
counting, spatial relations, or attribute binding (Appendix~\ref{app:cfg}).

\subsubsection{A modality-agnostic pipeline}\label{sec:fullpipeline}
Only $\tau_\eta$ depends on the modality; the autoencoder, the U-Net, and
the attention layers are shared \cite[Sec.~3.3]{rombach2022ldm}. For
\emph{text}, $\tau_\eta$ is a transformer or pretrained language encoder
with one token per word piece. For a \emph{class label}, a learned
embedding yields a single token ($m=1$), as in our ImageNet LDM. For a
\emph{layout}, bounding boxes with class labels are tokenized and
encoded. \emph{Spatially aligned} inputs, such as semantic maps,
low-resolution images, or inpainting masks, can instead be concatenated
with $z_t$ along the channel axis. With \emph{no condition}, as in our
LSUN Churches model, $\tau_\eta$ is dropped. LDM evaluates all of these
settings \cite[Secs.~4.3--4.5]{rombach2022ldm}.

Figure~\ref{fig:ldm} assembles the pipeline. \emph{Training}: train the
autoencoder once and freeze it; encode an image, add noise to its latent,
encode the paired condition with $\tau_\eta$, and train the conditional
U-Net with Eq.~\eqref{eq:ldmloss}. \emph{Generation}: draw
$z_T\sim\Normal(0,\Id)$, encode $y$ once, run the sampler with the
conditional (and, with CFG, unconditional) predictions, and decode once;
no real image or encoder is needed. One autoencoder serves many
conditions \cite[Sec.~1]{rombach2022ldm}.

\subsection{Published evidence on quality and speed}\label{sec:benchmark}
For a quantitative comparison within one task, Rombach
et al.~\cite[Table~6]{rombach2022ldm} report $0.26$ versus $0.97$
samples/s at $256^2$ and FID@2k $24.74$ versus $15.21$ after six epochs
for pixel LDM-1 and latent LDM-4 (KL with attention), respectively,
in Places inpainting. FID compares generated and reference feature
distributions \cite{heusel2017fid}. These figures describe the paper's
inpainting setup.

\paragraph{DDPM objective ablation.}
On unconditional CIFAR-10, Ho et al.~\cite[Tables~1--2]{ho2020ddpm}
report training-reference FID of $13.51$ for the variational objective
and $3.17$ for the simplified objective with epsilon prediction and
fixed isotropic variance. The reported NLL upper bound changes from
$3.70$ to $3.75$ bits per dimension.

\subsection{Supplementary text prompt results}\label{sec:prompt-results}
Changing the requested bus color produced a green bus at all four
seeds. None of those images clearly showed the requested blue passenger
car. Changing the requested side yielded no clear left-to-right reversal

identity or spatial relations fail. Appendix~\ref{app:prompt-grid}

\subsection{Training data, copyright, and bias}\label{sec:ethics}
Training data limits the subjects and variations a model learns.
The church checkpoints specialize in church imagery, and ImageNet
conditioning uses a fixed class vocabulary. Text-conditioned models
also depend on the attributes and relations described in captions.
Bianchi et al.~\cite{bianchi2023bias} found demographic stereotypes
in outputs from ordinary prompts. The vehicle prompts in our demo
do not assess demographic bias. Carlini et al.~\cite{carlini2023extract}
extracted memorized training images from diffusion models. Data provenance
and potential reproduction matter for copyright. The U.S. Copyright
Office discusses use of protected works in training under U.S. law
\cite{usco2025training}.
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
Learning to remove noise enables generation by fitting a sequence of
reverse conditional distributions. The 2015 diffusion framework supplies
forward Gaussian corruption, learned reverse mean/covariance functions,
and variational training against tractable posterior targets. Starting
from a new Gaussian draw and composing the reverse transitions then
produces an image sample. DDPM retains this construction while fixing
variance choices, parameterizing the reverse mean through predicted
noise, and simplifying the training objective. Its published ablation
supports a sample-quality benefit from that objective choice, distinct
from likelihood optimization or fewer sampling steps. The score
connection explains the parameterization without adding a separate
computational stage.


LDM preserves this denoising principle in a learned representation.
The ImageNet traces show recognizable structure emerging in both spaces.
In our LSUN Churches run, the latent pipeline had lower sampling latency
and higher peak tensor allocation. These measurements apply to the tested
checkpoints. The supplementary text demonstration shows changes in
bus color and persistent failures on object identity and spatial layout.
